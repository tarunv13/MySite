Growing up · Udaipur

By the Aravalli hills

I grew up in Udaipur, near the Aravalli hills, and watched the forests and lakes around the city being broken into smaller pieces. At school I joined the environmental awareness campaigns, which was the first time I tried to get other people to care about something I cared about.

Childhood · Ranthambhore

A photograph and a forest

My uncle was a forest official and a photographer. His exhibitions of the elusive wild cats of Ranthambhore Tiger Reserve showed both the beauty of the forest and how fragile it was, and they were my first sense that a photograph and a forest could belong to the same life.

2012 to 2016 · Rajasthan Technical University

Computer science

I studied computer science: programming and databases, the base for my computational work. At the same time I was photographing weddings, travel and streets, and the camera slowly took over.

2015 to 2018 · New Delhi

Raghu Rai’s magazine

I joined Creative Image Magazine, Raghu Rai’s photography magazine, which was meant to publish lesser-known photographers across South Asia. I built its community to more than 50,000 followers and over a million uses of its hashtag.

Working alongside Raghu Rai of Magnum Photos, I learned to read an image for its framing, its timing and what it leaves out, and to ask what conditions produced it. Nick Brandt’s photographs of disappearing wildlife showed me what that way of looking could do for conservation.

2016 to 2019 · across India

My own practice

For three years I ran my own photography practice, shooting destination weddings across India, and then led a team of ten on digital campaigns for clients in the UK, Europe and North America.

It was not research work, but it taught me to deliver under my own direction, and it funded a change of field into a discipline I had no formal training in.

2019 to 2021 · University of Kota

The record, not the forest

In 2019 I wrote under one of my photographs that our wildlife was “on the brink of extinction, at an unimaginable rate”, and that I was starting a master’s in wildlife science “to study for a cause and be part of something meaningful”.

My M.Sc. in Wildlife Science took me into fieldwork across protected areas. When COVID-19 closed the planned fieldwork, I worked with the enforcement record instead: 1,341 recorded offences from Ranthambhore, obtained from the forest department by field visit.

Recorded offences fell from 225 in 2019 to 98 in 2020, and the reading was not that offending had stopped but that cases were no longer being registered. The data produced the question before I could name it.

2020 · Haryana

What people already know

For the People’s Biodiversity Register in Haryana, I interviewed more than 200 households about the plants and practices they know, working through translators and local permissions .

It was my first work with traditional ecological knowledge, and I have wanted to go back to it ever since.

2021 to 2023 · WCCB and WCS-India

The trade in other words

As a volunteer analyst for the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, and then at WCS-India, I was shocked by how large the online market for wildlife was. While building keyword lists, I found that the same trade looked different depending on the language I searched in: a species that appeared absent in English was openly advertised in Hindi.

With Hindi and Telugu speakers, I built a lexicon of more than 50 codewords. A thousand videos had to be labelled one at a time before any could train the classifier.

2022 to 2024 · IIT Bombay

Who tells the story

With Prof. Pankaj Sekhsaria and Kalpavriksh’s Protected Area Update, I coded more than 1,000 reports from three decades of conservation news and helped build a public, mapped website for them.

The same protected area could appear as a conservation success in one report and through displacement or livelihood loss in another, depending on who was telling the story. On campus I joined the birdwatching group and helped run wildlife talks and nature walks for people who were not already interested.

2024 to 2025 · ATREE, with the University of Oxford

A programme that works like a game

I led the India component of the evaluation of UNEP’s Tide Turners Plastic Challenge, under Dr Diogo Veríssimo and Dr Saloni Bhatia. I ran fieldwork in states where I did not speak the local language, and it worked because I recruited through local partners who had the language and the standing.

Participants recycled more and talked more about plastic. But in Barmer, a teacher climbed a tree to find enough phone signal to register her students, and the record showed only the people who could reach the system.

Since October 2025 · independent

Tools I needed and could not find

Since my ATREE contract ended I have worked on my own projects: WildTrace, Sift QDA, Species on Screen and Kosma, and a paper with Dr Veríssimo on conservation NFTs. Each one is a tool I needed and could not find for free, so I built it and gave it away.

I am now looking for doctoral research on games, media and behaviour for nature.

Next · every continent

The life list

Photography and cycling are how I spend time outside work. The long plan is to see as many forms of life on Earth as I can, and to learn from the people who have known them longest, often in languages that are not mine.

Traditional knowledge holds a lot of what science is still catching up on. I want to learn from it with care, and keep what I make free and open, so that more people can look after the living world.

The window seat

On road trips and long train rides I am the one at the window, watching green country go past and looking for birds before they are gone.

In the kitchen

I learn a place through its food, and a shared recipe is often the easiest way into a conversation.
