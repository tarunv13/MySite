Work · 2019 to now

Work

I keep returning to one question. When a record tells us something happened, how far can we trust it, and who never made it into the record at all?

It began in Ranthambhore. Recorded offences fell by more than half in 2020, and the likelier story was that cases had stopped being registered during the pandemic, not that people had stopped offending. Since then I have read every dataset the same way. I want to know who made it, in which language, and who had no way to reach it.

Some of this work I did inside larger teams, and I learned a great deal from the people who led them. The rest are tools I built myself, because I could not find a free one that did the job. Under each project you will find who was involved, what I did and what we learned.

UNEP Tide Turners Plastic Challenge, India

Can a school programme that plays like a game change what young people actually do about plastic? And who does it never reach?

Tide Turners is the UN Environment Programme’s youth programme on plastic pollution, delivered in India through WWF-India and the Centre for Environment Education. It works like a game. Young people earn points for tasks, upload evidence to an online platform and move up from Entry to Leader to Champion badges. The evaluation asked whether that changes behaviour, and not only knowledge.

What I did

  • Secured permission from schools across six states, explaining the study to head teachers and programme coordinators before any data was collected.
  • Designed and translated the multilingual survey instruments and interview protocols, and trained two interns to interview in their own languages.
  • Ran a randomised pre-post comparison in Delhi (272 students) and Gujarat (392), from nearly 2,000 surveys collected, with 56 interviews in four regional languages.
  • Analysed the surveys with inferential statistics, coded the interviews thematically in NVivo, and drafted the report and the first-authored manuscript.
  • Coordinated regional teams, travel and budget, and produced the donor-facing documentation.

What we found

Participants were ahead of non-participants on household recycling by 55%, on conversations with friends about plastic by 35%, on recognising the problem by 23%, on personal responsibility by 22% and on recycling knowledge by 21%. The finding that mattered most was never in the design. Sitting with schools, I saw barriers separating how the programme was written from how it reached children, which internal monitoring had not surfaced. In Barmer, a teacher climbed a tree in the school yard to find enough phone signal to register her students .

  • 664students in the trial
  • +55%household recycling
  • 56interviews, 4 languages
  • 6states
Why it matters

Programmes that ask young people to change their habits are funded on the strength of their evidence. This evaluation showed where Tide Turners worked and who it could not reach, and those findings became recommendations on participant retention and fair access.

Built on

When we read about a wildlife seizure, how much do we really know about where the animal came from, or where it was going?

WildTrace is an open atlas of illegal wildlife trade in animals and plants. It maps seizures, arrests and convictions around the world and grades every case by the strength of its evidence, so that a rumour and a conviction never look the same on the map. It grew out of my years of monitoring trade by hand: a news report of a seizure tells you where someone was caught, and very little about where the animal came from.

What I did

  • Built a Python pipeline that collects public reports every day, merges duplicates and grades each case from a single report up to an official or validated record.
  • Set the cases against 15,385 seized shipments from the CITES Trade Database (2015 to 2024), so the routes from supply to demand are visible.
  • Added a zoonoses view that places host species next to the trade, and is careful to show co-location without claiming cause.
  • Built an in-browser link-analysis workbench, so an analyst without a commercial licence can still follow a network, and published every release on Zenodo with a DOI.

What it shows

Seizure records are dominated by the countries that report the most, so the atlas shows reporting effort next to the cases. It names no individuals: the question is the trade, not the people caught in it .

  • 1,200+graded cases, updated daily
  • 15,385seized shipments, CITES 2015–2024
  • Freeopen data and code
Why it matters

Journalists, researchers and small NGOs can follow the trade with the kind of analysis enforcement agencies pay for, and can see how strong the evidence is behind each case before they repeat it.

Kalpavriksh’s Protected Area Update

Partners

For thirty years, local reporters have written about India’s protected areas. Whose stories made it into print?

Since the 1990s, Kalpavriksh’s Protected Area Update has reported news from India’s national parks and sanctuaries, from tiger deaths to turtle nesting on the Odisha coast. It is a near three-decade record of more than 6,000 reports, and it deserved to be searchable and mapped.

What I did

  • Designed the thematic coding framework and coded more than 1,000 reports in NVivo for how the news constructs conservation, human–wildlife interaction and governance.
  • Built a research-grade geospatial database in QGIS and the tagging and visualisation workflow behind the public website.
  • Sketched how the website’s data repository should work. You filter by legal status or theme, scroll a list of stories, and open each one on the map where it happened .
  • Presented the work at the Central India Landscape Symposium in Kanha and at the Student Conference on Conservation Science, IISc Bengaluru, both in 2023.

What we found

The same protected area could appear as a conservation success in one report and through displacement or livelihood loss in another, depending on who was telling the story.

  • 6,000+reports in the archive
  • 1,000+coded by me
  • 1994–2023years of news
Why it matters

Anyone can now search and map three decades of local conservation reporting, and that includes the communities the stories are about.

Why should careful qualitative research need an expensive licence, or send sensitive interviews to someone else’s server?

I coded my Tide Turners interviews and the Protected Area Update archive in NVivo, and a licence like that is out of reach for most students and small organisations in India. Sift QDA is my answer: a free qualitative data analysis app that codes interviews, PDFs and survey answers without the data ever leaving your computer.

What it does

  • Codes interviews, documents, PDFs and survey answers, with memos and a codebook.
  • Searches by meaning offline, so asking for “fear of losing income” finds the person who said they did not know how they would pay rent.
  • Moves projects to and from NVivo, ATLAS.ti and MAXQDA through the REFI-QDA standard, and exports every coded extract to Excel in one click.
Why it matters

Students and NGOs can do careful research without paying for a licence, and interviews with sensitive details stay on their own machine, which is often what an ethics board asks for.

No. 05 · Evidence-gated AI

Kosma

  • Code
Years
2026
My role
Creator. MIT licence.
Keywords
  • trustworthy AI
  • explainability
  • privacy
  • Swiss Ephemeris

What would it take for an AI to admit what it does not know?

Kosma is a privacy-first Vedic astrology engine, and a side project about how to make an AI honest. The ephemeris computes a birth chart to arc-second precision, and the language model only interprets what the chart can support.

How it works

  • Any reading that two independent chart factors do not confirm is withheld, and listed as withheld.
  • Every line of the report traces back to its arithmetic and its source text, in an evidence ledger.
  • Nothing a user enters is stored: it is computed in memory and discarded.
Why it matters

It is a small working example of an AI that says what it cannot support, and that design choice matters far beyond astrology. It is the same rule I use in research: say what the record supports, and be open about what it does not.

2021 to 2023 · Trade monitoring

Online wildlife trade in Indian languages

Role
Technical Assistant, Counter Wildlife Trafficking programme, WCS-India (2022, 2023). Volunteer analyst, Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (2021 to 2022).
Keywords
  • codewords
  • Hindi and Telugu
  • text classification
  • YouTube Data API

I built and ran the programme’s online monitoring pipeline, with a Python collection layer on the YouTube Data API. The keyword lists were in English, and the trade was not. With Hindi and Telugu speakers inside and outside the organisation, I compiled a lexicon of more than 50 trade codewords, mapping each to its species and product, and credited the people who gave me the terms.

I hand-labelled more than 1,000 videos and trained a text classifier that reached 87% accuracy. The number I learned most from was a different one. It caught only 43% of the irrelevant clips, because four-fifths of my labels were positive. As a volunteer with the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, I tracked suspected trade on e-commerce and social media and submitted case reports to its enforcement officer for CITES.

Why it matters

Monitoring that works in the languages the trade actually uses finds what English-only systems miss.

Species on Screen asks how digital nature content, from documentaries to video games, shapes the way people engage with biodiversity. It pairs a quiet, cinematic reading surface with a research archive of attested observations, one place, one species and one source at a time. A first case study follows how people talked about beavers on YouTube before and after Pixar’s Hoppers (2026).

Why it matters

2019 to 2021 · M.Sc. dissertation

Wildlife offences in Ranthambhore

Role
M.Sc. Wildlife Science, University of Kota. Supervised by Dr Satya Prakash Mehra; co-supervised by Dr Areendran Gopala (IGCMC, WWF-India).
Keywords
  • enforcement records
  • QGIS heatmaps
  • surveillance technology

With the Field Director’s permission, I visited Ranthambhore Tiger Reserve to collect its enforcement records. They held 1,341 recorded offences from January 2015 to May 2021, in six categories from human disturbance and wood cutting to hunting. I georeferenced the patrol posts by GPS, mapped the offences as heatmaps in QGIS and assessed five surveillance technologies, from camera traps to M-STrIPES, against the data each returns.

Recorded offences fell from 225 in 2019 to 98 in 2020. The honest reading was that cases had stopped being registered under COVID-19, and I had no second record to set against it. That gap became the question behind everything on this page.
