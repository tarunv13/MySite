Open atlas · 2026 to now

WildTrace

Creator and maintainer · open source, MIT licence

WildTrace is an open atlas of illegal wildlife trade. It maps seizures, arrests and convictions around the world, in animals and in plants , and grades every case by the strength of its evidence, so that a rumour and a conviction never look the same on the map.

It grew out of my years of monitoring trade by hand. A news report of a pangolin seizure tells you where someone was caught, and very little about where the animal came from. So WildTrace sets the cases against trade flows from the CITES Trade Database, and shows plainly where reporting is heavy and where it is thin. A zoonoses view places host species next to the trade, and it is careful to show co-location without claiming cause.

Everything refreshes daily, the data can be downloaded, and a link-analysis workbench runs in the browser, so an analyst without a commercial licence can still follow a network.

Impact evaluation · Oct 2024 to Oct 2025

Tide Turners Plastic Challenge, India

Senior Research Associate, ATREE, with the University of Oxford · led the India component

Tide Turners is UNEP’s youth programme on plastic, and it works like a game: young people earn points for tasks, upload evidence to an online platform and move up from Entry to Leader to Champion badges. Under principal investigators Dr Diogo Veríssimo (University of Oxford) and Dr Saloni Bhatia (ATREE), I led the India component of its evaluation as the sole field researcher.

I secured permission from schools across six states, translated the questionnaire, trained two interns to interview in their own languages and did the thematic analysis myself. We collected nearly 2,000 surveys, and the usable responses gave a randomised pre-post sample of 664 students in Delhi and Gujarat, with 56 interviews in four regional languages.

Participants were ahead of non-participants on household recycling by 55% and on conversations with friends about plastic by 35%. What the platform could not show was who had never been able to play. In Barmer, a teacher climbed a tree in the school yard to find enough phone signal to register her students. That finding went into recommendations on retention and fair access, and it is the question I want to take further in games research.

Archive and portal · Sept 2022 to 2024

Protected Area Update database

Research Assistant, C-TARA, IIT Bombay, with Kalpavriksh · under the guidance of Prof. Pankaj Sekhsaria

Since the 1990s, the Protected Area Update newsletter from Kalpavriksh has recorded news from India’s national parks and sanctuaries, from tiger deaths to olive ridley nesting on the Odisha coast. It is a near three-decade record of more than 6,000 reports.

I turned that archive into a research-grade geospatial database in QGIS. I designed the coding framework, coded more than 1,000 reports for how the news constructs conservation, human–wildlife interaction and governance, and built the geospatial tagging and visualisation workflow behind the public portal. Having seen how differently the same events could be told, I moved on to testing whether behaviour change programmes work.

I presented this work at the Central India Landscape Symposium in Kanha (2023) and at the Student Conference on Conservation Science at IISc Bengaluru (2023). In 2024 I stayed on at IIT Bombay for a short study of bamboo plantation for watershed restoration in semi-arid Maharashtra.

Trade monitoring · 2022 to 2023

Online wildlife trade in Indian languages

Technical Assistant, Counter Wildlife Trafficking programme, WCS-India

I built and ran the programme’s online monitoring pipeline, with a Python collection layer on the YouTube Data API. The keyword lists were in English, and the trade was not. With Hindi and Telugu speakers inside and outside the organisation, I compiled a lexicon of more than 50 trade codewords, mapping each one to its species and product, and credited the people who gave me the terms.

I hand-labelled more than 1,000 videos and trained a text classifier that reached 87% accuracy. The number I learned most from was a different one: it caught only 43% of the irrelevant clips, because four-fifths of my labels were positive. Building the model was the easy half, and the labels were the expertise.

Alongside this I volunteered as an analyst with the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, submitting case reports on online trade to its enforcement officer for CITES.

Media observatory · ongoing

Species on Screen

Independent research · open source

Species on Screen asks how digital nature content, from documentaries to video games, shapes the way people engage with biodiversity. It pairs a quiet, cinematic reading surface with a research archive of attested observations, one place, one species and one source at a time.

Attention is easy to count and hard to read. A species that trends online is not always a species that people then act for, and this project is my way of measuring that gap with care.

Open-source app · 2026

Sift QDA

Creator · MIT or Apache-2.0

I coded my Tide Turners interviews in NVivo, and a licence like that is out of reach for most students and small organisations in India. Sift QDA is my answer: a free qualitative data analysis app for Windows that codes interviews, PDFs and survey answers without the data ever leaving your computer.

It searches by meaning offline, so asking for “fear of losing income” finds the person who said they did not know how they would pay rent. Projects move to and from NVivo, ATLAS.ti and MAXQDA through the REFI-QDA standard, and one click exports every coded extract to Excel.

Evidence-gated AI · 2026

Kosma

Creator · MIT licence

Kosma is a privacy-first Vedic astrology engine and a side project about how to make an AI honest. The ephemeris computes a birth chart to arc-second precision, and the language model only interprets what the chart can support. Any reading that two independent chart factors do not confirm is withheld and listed as withheld, and every line can be traced back to its arithmetic and its source text.

It is a long way from wildlife, but the rule is the same one I use in research: say what the record supports, and be open about what it does not. Nothing a user enters is stored.

M.Sc. dissertation · 2019 to 2021

Wildlife offences in Ranthambhore

University of Kota · supervised by Dr Satya Prakash Mehra, co-supervised by Dr Areendran Gopala (WWF-India)

With the Field Director’s permission, I obtained Ranthambhore Tiger Reserve’s enforcement records by field visit: 1,341 recorded offences from January 2015 to May 2021, in six categories from human disturbance and wood cutting to hunting. I georeferenced the patrol posts by GPS, mapped the offences as heatmaps in QGIS and assessed five surveillance technologies, from camera traps to M-STrIPES, against the data each returns.

Recorded offences fell from 225 in 2019 to 98 in 2020. The honest reading was that cases had stopped being registered under COVID-19, and I had no second record to set against it. That gap became the question behind everything on this page.
